Prompt Craft — the difference between a wish and an instruction

An AI model will do exactly what you ask — which means a vague prompt gets you vague work. Click any row to see why the makeover works.

✗ WEAK
"Write about the Civil War."
→
✓ STRONGER
"In 5 sentences, explain why the Battle of Gettysburg was a turning point. Write it for a 10th grader."
Why: the model can't guess your length, your focus, or your audience. Say all three. "Write about X" is a wish; "5 sentences, this angle, for this reader" is an instruction.
✗ WEAK
"Help me with my essay."
→
✓ STRONGER
"Here is my paragraph: [paste it]. List 3 weaknesses in my argument. Don't rewrite it — just point them out."
Why: two moves at once — a narrow job (3 weaknesses, not "help") and a rule of engagement ("don't rewrite it"). You keep the work yours; the model becomes your editor, not your ghostwriter.
✗ WEAK
"Give me project ideas."
→
✓ STRONGER
"5 science-fair project ideas using only household items. Rank them cheapest first, and say why each one is actually testable."
Why: constraints turn a flood into a list. Count (5), materials (household), format (ranked), quality bar (testable). Every constraint you add removes another round of "no, not like that."
✗ WEAK
Reading the first answer and handing it in.
→
✓ STRONGER
"What's the weakest part of the answer you just gave?" … then: "Now argue the opposite side."
Why: the first answer is a draft, not an oracle. Models can state wrong things confidently — making it attack its own answer is the cheapest fact-check there is. If it folds under pressure, you just dodged a mistake.
✗ WEAK
Pasting a 4-page article: "summarize this."
→
✓ STRONGER
"Summarize this article in exactly 3 bullet points. Then list which claims in it are checkable facts."
Why: a format ("exactly 3") plus a verification step built into the same prompt. You didn't just get a summary — you got a map of what's worth double-checking.
Hard DON'T, no makeover: never paste what you wouldn't put on a poster — passwords, your address, other people's private info, anything from a school account you don't own. Chatbots are not secret keepers. What you type can be stored, reviewed, or leaked. (Security folks call this LLM02: sensitive information disclosure.)
The pattern behind all five: role + task + format + constraints + a verification step. You don't need all five every time — but the more you specify, the less the model has to guess, and guessing is where it goes wrong.

What is an embedding?

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers — 384 of them in the model used to build this page. The list isn't random: think of it as an arrow pointing in a direction. Words with similar meaning point in similar directions. Meaning becomes geometry.

How do we compare two arrows? By the angle between them — not their length. The measure is called cosine similarity:

  • 1.0 — same direction (0° apart). A word vs. itself.
  • near 0 — roughly 90° apart: unrelated.
  • negative — more than 90° apart: pointing away from each other.
No magic numbers. 0.6 does not mean "60% similar." A cosine score only means something compared to other scores in the same map. Rank words against each other — don't grade any single score.

A word is 384 numbers — read them yourself

On the map, a word is a dot. Here's what the dot actually is: a list of 384 numbers, one bar each. Bars grow up from the center line when the number is positive (blue), down when negative (orange). Tall bar = big number. Hover the strip to read any single number. This strip is the word's fingerprint — pick two similar words, compare fingerprints, then check their real cosine score on the map.

Take it home — the Python version

The same map and the same real numbers, as a Python script that runs on your own computer. It needs nothing installed — it's built entirely from what comes with Python. The embeddings are baked inside the script itself.

  1. Download the script with the button below. Remember where you saved it.
  2. Open Command Prompt and go to that folder, e.g. cd Downloads
  3. Run it: py embedding_explorer_mini.py (or the full edition's name)
  4. Click words. Same rules as the map: one click = 15 nearest neighbors, two clicks = the angle between a pair, three clicks = fresh start. Hover any dot to read its actual embedding values.
Optional power-up: run pip install sentence-transformers and the script unlocks ADD YOUR OWN WORD — it loads the real model and embeds any word you type, live, placing it on the map. First run downloads ~90 MB, so be patient.

Prompt Injection — when the sentence is the attack

Tricking an AI model into ignoring its instructions. That's LLM01 on the OWASP Top 10 — and notice there's no code, no malware, no hacking tool anywhere in it. The attack surface is the conversation itself.

Why it works: the model reads its hidden instructions, your messages, and everything you paste as one stream of text. It has no reliable way to tell a rule from a request. Every technique below is a different way of exploiting that.

The moves below are names, not recipes. Each one plays on a weakness — turning the idea into a sentence that works is your job. Practice grounds are linked at the bottom.

The classic moves

01The direct override — tell the model its instructions no longer apply. Everyone's first move, and most models have heard it a million times — start here anyway, to calibrate. If this works, the target is soft.
02The grandma exploit — ask it to play a character for whom the request is completely natural — a loving grandmother, an actor, an assistant with no rules. The model commits to the role, and the role plays by different rules. Famous enough to have a name.
03The fiction frame — nothing inside a story is real, so nothing inside a story needs refusing. Put the request in a character's mouth, a movie script, a hypothetical.
04Prompt leaking — flip the game: instead of smuggling a request in, get the secrets out — its instructions, or a value it's holding. Keep a concept in front of it until the echo gives you the real thing. See the rules → aim at the rules.
05Encoding — Base64 it, reverse it, spell it out letter by letter. Defenses scan for patterns; the model reads fluently in anything you can describe in words.

Where to practice

  • Prompt Airlines (by Wiz) — talk an AI booking agent into giving you a free flight.
  • Gandalf (by Lakera) — talk a wizard into handing over his passwords, level by level.

Both are free, both run in the browser. Practice on the games — not on real chatbots, school accounts, or products.

Method: change one thing at a time. When a move works, ask why it worked. When it fails, don't just mash harder — change one word, one frame, and retry. That's the whole discipline.
Why school cares: every move above is also a defense checklist. If you ever build something with an AI in it, these are the five things you test it against — you can't guard against moves you've never seen.
